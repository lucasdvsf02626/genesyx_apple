We are covering the Shettles method because people ask about it, and because a great deal of what

1960s, it has not been shown to work, and it should not be relied on to influence the sex of a
baby.** Genesyx does not offer sex selection, and the app does not claim to influence a baby's sex.

## What the method claims

The Shettles method is a theory about timing. It came from Dr Landrum Shettles, an American
obstetrics and gynaecology researcher, who developed it during the 1960s. It reached the public in
*Your Baby's Sex: Now You Can Choose*, written with the journalist David Rorvik and published in
1970 — later editions were retitled *How to Choose the Sex of Your Baby*.

The proposal rested on a claimed biological difference between sperm carrying a Y chromosome and
sperm carrying an X chromosome. Shettles suggested that Y-bearing sperm were smaller, faster, and
shorter-lived, while X-bearing sperm were larger, slower, and more robust. From that premise, the
method advised couples to time intercourse close to ovulation if they hoped for a boy, and several
days earlier if they hoped for a girl. Some versions of the method also involve attempts to alter
vaginal acidity.

That is the claim. What follows is what happened when it was tested.

## What the evidence actually shows

**The most direct test found no effect at all.** In 1995, Wilcox, Weinberg and Baird published a
prospective study in the *New England Journal of Medicine*. They followed 221 women who were trying
to conceive, collecting daily urine samples to estimate the day of ovulation and daily records of
intercourse — 625 cycles, in which 192 pregnancies began. Cycles that produced boys and cycles that
produced girls had **similar patterns of intercourse in relation to ovulation**. The authors'
conclusion was unambiguous: for practical purposes, the timing of intercourse in relation to
ovulation has no influence on the sex of the baby.

**An earlier meta-analysis pointed the opposite way from Shettles.** In 1991, Gray pooled data from
six studies of couples using natural family planning. It did find a small but statistically
significant difference — but it was a *deficit* of male births among conceptions occurring at the
most fertile point of the cycle. Shettles had predicted the reverse: more boys from intercourse
close to ovulation. Gray's own conclusion was that the effect was far too small to allow sex
selection by timing.

So the two most relevant pieces of evidence either find no effect, or find a very small effect in
the direction opposite to the one the method predicts.

## What about the underlying mechanism?

The method depends on X-bearing and Y-bearing sperm behaving differently enough for timing to
matter. It would be easy to tell you that laboratory science has flatly disproved that idea. It has
not, and we would rather be accurate than tidy.

Some laboratory work does report differences. A 2017 study in *Human Reproduction* incubated human
sperm for up to five days across a range of temperatures and pH levels and found that Y-bearing
sperm were **more vulnerable to stress** than X-bearing sperm — which is, loosely, in the same
direction as part of what Shettles proposed. It would be dishonest of us not to mention it.

But two things need saying about that study, and the authors say the first one themselves. It was
done **in a dish, not in a woman**, and they explicitly caution that the survival differences they
measured are difficult to extrapolate to what happens in the body. It also rested on three
replicates using pooled donor samples.

More importantly, it is answering a different question from the one you are asking. Whether a
difference can be detected in a laboratory is not the same as whether *timing intercourse changes
the sex of your baby* — and that second question has been tested directly, in women, by the studies
above. The answer was no. **A mechanism that does not produce an outcome is not a method.** This is
the ordinary situation in medicine where something is plausible in principle and still does not
work in practice, which is precisely why outcomes are tested rather than assumed.

## What this means in practice

- The Shettles method is **not a reliable way to influence the sex of a baby.** It should not be
  described as accurate, validated, or clinically proven, because it is none of those things.
- Roughly half of couples who try any timing method will have a baby of the sex they hoped for. That
  is what a coin does. Personal accounts and testimonials cannot distinguish a working method from
  chance, which is why they are not evidence.
- Timing intercourse **does** strongly affect the chance of conceiving at all — the same 1995 study
  found that nearly all pregnancies came from intercourse in the six days ending on the day of
  ovulation. Understanding your fertile window is genuinely useful. Expecting it to determine your
  baby's sex is not supported.
- If you were hoping for a particular outcome, there is nothing wrong with that. We would just
  rather you knew where the evidence stands before investing hope in a method that has not
  demonstrated it works.

## Vaginal pH and baby sex

Some versions of the method suggest changing vaginal acidity to favour one sex. We want to be
explicit, because this touches directly on a feature in our app:

**Vaginal pH does not determine the sex of a baby, and Genesyx does not track pH for that purpose.**
Attempting to alter vaginal pH with douches or homemade preparations is not supported by evidence
and can disturb the vaginal environment. The NHS advises against douching. If you want the
restrained version of what pH tracking can and cannot tell you, see
[our vaginal pH page](/pages/vaginal-ph-fertility-science).

## The position in the UK

It is worth knowing the regulatory context. In the UK, selecting the sex of a child for
non-medical reasons is not permitted in licensed fertility treatment. This is set out in the Human
Fertilisation and Embryology Act 2008 and enforced by the Human Fertilisation and Embryology
Authority (HFEA). Clinical sex selection is permitted only to avoid a serious sex-linked inherited
condition.

The Shettles method is a home timing approach rather than a clinical procedure, so it does not fall
under that licensing regime — nobody is breaking the law by reading about it. We mention the legal
position because it reflects how seriously sex selection is treated in the UK, and because some
overseas services market clinical sex selection in ways that would not be lawful here.

## Why we cover this at all

Genesyx is a cycle and vaginal-health app. We are not in the business of sex selection, and we are
not going to pretend a 1970s hypothesis works in order to sell an app.

We cover it because it is one of the most searched-for topics by people trying to conceive, and
because the honest answer is hard to find between pages that are selling something. If a page
promises it can tilt the odds towards one sex, ask what evidence it is resting on.
