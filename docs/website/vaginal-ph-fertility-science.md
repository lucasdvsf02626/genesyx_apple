## What vaginal pH actually is

pH is a measure of how acidic or alkaline something is, on a scale from 0 to 14. Below 7 is acidic,
7 is neutral, above 7 is alkaline.

The vagina is normally acidic. That acidity is largely produced by *Lactobacillus* bacteria, which
make lactic acid as part of their ordinary metabolism. The current understanding is that this acidic
environment is one of several factors that make it harder for other organisms to establish
themselves.

This is a description of an environment. It is not a fertility score, and it is not a measure of
how healthy you are as a person.

## The usual range — and why one number is not a verdict

In reproductive-age women, vaginal pH is commonly described as sitting somewhere around
**3.8 to 4.5**. A reading above 4.5 is one of the four things clinicians look at when assessing
for bacterial vaginosis, alongside the appearance of discharge, its smell, and what is seen under
a microscope.
Note that it is *one of four* — pH alone is not how the condition is diagnosed.

It is worth being honest about the limits of that range. In a study of 396 asymptomatic North
American women, average vaginal pH differed meaningfully between ethnic groups: roughly 4.2 in white
women, 4.4 in Asian women, 4.7 in black women, and 5.0 in Hispanic women. All of these women were
without symptoms. The authors argued directly for a more refined definition of what counts as a
normal vaginal bacterial community.

**What that means for you in practical terms:** a reading above 4.5 is not automatically a problem,
and a reading below it is not a clean bill of health. A single number, taken out of context and away
from how you actually feel, is weak evidence on its own.

## Why readings move around

Vaginal pH is not a fixed personal constant. It shifts for ordinary reasons, including:

- **Where you are in your cycle.** Hormonal changes across the month affect the vaginal environment.
- **Your period.** Menstrual blood is close to neutral, so readings taken during or just after
  bleeding often come back higher.
- **Recent sex.** Semen is alkaline, so a reading taken soon afterwards can be raised for reasons
  that have nothing to do with your health.
- **Douching, washes, and internal products.** These can disturb the vaginal environment. The NHS
  advises against douching.
- **Life stage.** Vaginal pH tends to be higher before puberty and after menopause, when oestrogen
  levels are lower.
- **How the test was taken.** Strip placement, timing, and reading the colour by eye all introduce
  variation.

This is the single most important thing to understand about home tracking: **a lot of the movement
you will see is normal, and some of it is measurement noise.**

## What the evidence reasonably supports

- Vaginal pH is normally acidic, and that acidity is associated with *Lactobacillus*-dominant
  bacterial communities.
- A raised vaginal pH is **one** recognised clinical sign among several used when assessing for
  bacterial vaginosis.
- Bacterial vaginosis is common, is often symptomless, and is treatable. It is worth seeing a
  clinician about.
- What counts as a "normal" pH varies between individuals and between populations more than a single
  cut-off suggests.

## What is uncertain, and what we will not claim

We want to be straightforward about the parts that are not settled:

- **We do not claim that tracking your vaginal pH at home will help you conceive.** We are not aware
  of good evidence that home pH monitoring improves the chance of pregnancy, and we do not present
  it as a fertility intervention.
- **The link between vaginal bacteria and reproductive outcomes is still being worked out.** The
  research here is genuinely mixed. To take one example: a 2025 systematic review and network
  meta-analysis of 24 randomised trials found that treating bacterial vaginosis during pregnancy did
  **not** reduce preterm delivery overall, while other recent work has reported a benefit in
  higher-risk groups. That is an unresolved question, and it should be described as one.
- **Vaginal pH does not tell you anything about the sex of a future baby.** See our separate page on
  [the Shettles method](/pages/shettles-method-evidence-limitations).
- **A home strip is not a diagnostic test.** It does not identify an organism, and it cannot
  distinguish between the many different reasons a reading might be raised.

## What home tracking may be useful for — and how confident we are

We should be clear about the status of this section: what follows is our **reasoning for why the
feature exists**, not a demonstrated clinical benefit. We are not aware of trial evidence showing
that home pH tracking improves any health or fertility outcome, and we are not going to imply there
is some.

The argument is a modest one. Keeping your own record may help you notice a change from what is
usual *for you* — for example, that your readings tend to shift at a particular point in your cycle.
Knowing your own baseline is generally more informative than comparing yourself to a population
average, for the reasons set out above.

That is a prompt to pay attention, and potentially something concrete to mention at an appointment.
It is not a diagnosis, it is not a reason to treat yourself, and if it ever becomes a reason to
*delay* seeing someone, it has done you harm rather than good.

## Please speak to a clinician if

- You have unusual discharge, or discharge with an unfamiliar smell
- You have itching, soreness, burning, or pain — including pain during sex or when passing urine
- You have bleeding between periods or after sex
- You are pregnant and notice a change in discharge
- You keep getting symptoms that come back after treatment
- Something simply does not feel right to you

In the UK you can see a GP, go to a sexual health clinic — many take walk-ins and can test more
quickly — or speak to a pharmacist. Please do not use the app to decide whether or not you need to
be seen.

## How Genesyx handles pH

The app lets you record a reading, see your own history, and view a simple trend over time. It shows
where a reading sits relative to the commonly cited range and flags readings above 4.5 as worth
noticing.

It deliberately does not tell you that you have an infection, name a cause, recommend a treatment,
or predict a fertility outcome. It is a place to keep a record and spot a change — nothing more than
that.
